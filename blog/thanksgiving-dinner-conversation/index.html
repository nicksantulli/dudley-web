How to Keep Thanksgiving Dinner Conversation From Turning Into Politics (or Silence)

Written by Nicholas Santulli·Published October 13, 2026

Keep Thanksgiving dinner on the people in the room. Park a political comment without a lecture, ask one person the next question, and use prompts a kid and a grandparent can both answer.

Thanksgiving is November 26 this year. A big family dinner fails in two directions that feel the same from the head of the table. One is a fight about the news nobody came to have. The other is a silence so flat you can hear someone decide not to talk.

The job is a third thing. The people in the room, talking about the people in the room.

You do not get that by posting a rule on the wall. You get it by knowing the next question before you need it, and by having one sentence ready when the news walks in anyway.

Decide what the table is for

Say it once, before the plates are full. Short is better. “Tonight I want to hear about the people in this room.” That is a job, not a debate policy. A sign that says no politics is a joke until someone enforces it, and enforcement at dinner is just another argument.

You are allowed to have a point of view. Having one is not the same as handing the table to the loudest headline. The people who cooked, traveled, or went a year without this room are the subject. The news can wait until the dishes are done.

When politics shows up

It will, in some rooms. Redirect it. Do not take the bait, and do not give a speech about how families should behave.

“Let’s not get political” is a scolding. A scolding is how a spark becomes the main event. A short talk about listening is the same problem in a kinder voice. The table did not sit down for a panel.

Park it in one sentence, then give the next turn to a person.

  • “I’m going to park that. I want to hear about the thing you made this year, before I forget to ask.”
  • “You two can finish this in the kitchen after pie, if you still want to. I want a story from someone who hasn’t talked.”
  • “I hear you. I’m not going to settle it over the turkey.”

Say it to someone, with a next question attached. A question thrown at the whole room lets the same two people pick it up again.

If you are the one who wants to argue, you go last, or you do not go. The redirect fails the moment you take the other side.

If someone is actually hurt, a question will not fix it. Step away from the table with them.

A this-or-that game is a different tool, and it breaks the moment one option is a political side. Why Would You Rather Works at the Dinner Table is about that format. Use it after the room is already talking, not as a way to sneak the argument back in.

Questions for mixed ages

These work when a kid and a grandparent are at the same table. They ask for a dish, a habit, or a memory. They do not ask anyone to report their year or pick a side.

Skip “What are you thankful for?” unless someone offers it. It asks people to perform gratitude on cue.

  • What is a meal at this table you can still taste, even if nobody makes it the same way?
  • Who taught you one small kitchen thing you still do?
  • What is a family habit that made no sense when you were little and makes sense now, or still does not?
  • What did a holiday in this family smell like when you were small?
  • What is a dish you only eat when this family sits down together?
  • What is a game, a show, or a story this family repeats every year?

Stay with the answer you got. If they mention a burned pie, ask who burned it. Do not stack the next prompt until that one has had a lap around the table.

For the quiet seat

Some people will not volunteer. That is not a problem you solve by putting them on stage. Ask something they can finish in a sentence, and answer it yourself first if the table is stiff. Then stop.

  • What is a tool, a song, or a route you trust more than most people do?
  • What did you get good at because nobody else wanted the job?
  • Where do you go when the house gets too loud?
  • What is a road, a shop, or a song you will go out of your way for?
  • What is the first thing you notice when you walk into this kitchen?

One question. A second one, right away, is a spotlight. Leave them an exit.

After dessert

Dessert is late enough for a lighter question. Over the turkey, the same question would have sounded like a program. People are less careful with a fork in their hand.

  • What is a small tradition you would be sad to lose, even if it is a little ridiculous?
  • Who at this table is the person you call when something breaks?
  • What is a family story you want told correctly at least once?
  • What are you looking forward to before the next time we all sit down like this?
  • If this table had a mascot that is not a person, what would it be?

Save the mascot for the end. Asked at the start of dinner, it tells everyone you are running a game. Asked after pie, it is just pie.

The list is a pocket. Ask one question when the table needs a next thing. If people are already inside a story, leave the rest alone.

When the silence turns into phones

A comfortable pause is fine. A silence after a sharp comment is not a pause, and it can end with every person in their own screen.

That exit is a different problem from the fight. The Phone in the Middle of the Table is about one shared object instead of four private ones. This post is the sentence you use when the news shows up, and the questions you put on that object once the phone is face-up.

When you do not want to be the only one holding the list

You can run this dinner from a card in your pocket. You do not need an app to ask any of the questions above.

The awkward part is being the only person who thought of them, while you are also carrying the turkey. A card in the middle takes that job off your seat. Someone else can draw the next one. You go back to being a person at the table.

Frequently asked questions

How do you keep Thanksgiving dinner from turning into a political argument?

Decide out loud that the night is for the people in the room. When a political comment lands, do not score it and do not lecture. Park it in one sentence, then ask one specific person a question about a meal, a memory, or someone at the table.

What should you talk about at Thanksgiving dinner instead of politics?

Talk about the people and the meal. Good prompts ask for a dish you only eat with this family, a kitchen skill someone taught you, a holiday smell, a route or a tool a quiet person trusts, or a small tradition you would miss. Skip a round of performed gratitude.

How do you change the subject at dinner without lecturing?

Name the park, then hand the next turn to a person. One line is enough: you are leaving the news until later, and you want a story from someone who has not talked. A question aimed at the whole room lets the same two people pick the fight back up.
