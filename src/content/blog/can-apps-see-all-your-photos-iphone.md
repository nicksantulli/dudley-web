Apps cannot automatically see every photo on your iPhone. They can see your full library only when you grant full Photos access. For most one-photo workflows, you can choose a specific image, use Limited Access, or use the system picker so the rest of your camera roll stays private.

That distinction matters for photo rating apps, aura apps, editors, social apps, and any tool that asks you to upload a selfie. The privacy question is not simply "does this app use photos?" The better question is: **which photos did you give it, and what does the app say it does with them?**

## Why this question is coming up now

Photo privacy keeps coming up because more apps ask people to choose, edit, rate, organize, or remix personal photos. Apple says third-party apps can access photos only with permission, and its Photo Picker is designed so you can select specific photos without opening the rest of your library. That makes the privacy setting part of the product experience, not a footnote.

For AI photo generators, aura apps, selfie editors, and group-chat photo games, the best question is not whether the app is fun. It is whether the app asks for the narrowest photo access that still lets the feature work.

## The short answer

If an iPhone app asks for Photos permission, you usually have several choices:

- **Full Access:** the app can access your photo library and associated metadata.
- **Limited Access:** the app can access only the photos and videos you selected.
- **Add Photos Only:** the app can save to your library without reading existing photos, when the app supports that mode.
- **No Access:** the app cannot access your Photos library.
- **System picker selection:** the app receives only the item you selected through Apple's picker flow.

Apple's Photos privacy page says users can allow full access, limit access to selected photos and videos, or use Photo Picker from inside an app. It also notes that photo access can include metadata such as location and depth information. Apple's broader privacy feature page says photo pickers help you select which photos to share while keeping the rest of the library private.

Sources: [Apple Photos & Privacy](https://www.apple.com/legal/privacy/data/en/photos/) and [Apple Privacy Features](https://www.apple.com/privacy/features/).

## What Full Access means

Full Access is the broadest Photos permission. It is useful for apps that genuinely need to browse, organize, back up, or sync an entire photo library.

For a simple upload, rating, meme, aura, or share-card workflow, full-library access is worth questioning. The app may only need the one image you are actively choosing. When the permission prompt appears, pause long enough to check whether the app gives you a narrower option.

This is especially important because photos can carry context beyond the image itself: locations, dates, edit history, and other metadata may matter depending on what you share and which access mode you grant.

## What Limited Access protects

Limited Access is the practical middle ground. You can let an app use a chosen set of photos without opening the rest of your library.

Use it when:

- The app needs to reuse a few selected images.
- You are not comfortable granting full-library access.
- The app has a social, editing, AI, or entertainment feature that works from specific uploads.
- You want to test an app before trusting it with broader access.

You can change access later from iPhone Settings: **Privacy & Security > Photos**, then select the app. Apple Support also points users to Privacy & Security settings as the place to control which apps can access information such as Photos, Contacts, Camera, and Microphone.

Source: [Apple Support: Control what you share on iPhone](https://support.apple.com/guide/iphone/control-what-you-share-iph6e7d349d1/ios).

## Does a photo picker give an app your whole library?

Not by itself. A system picker is designed around user selection. You pick the photo or video, and the app gets the selected item rather than automatic access to everything.

That is the cleaner flow for many single-photo actions:

- Upload this receipt.
- Edit this picture.
- Rate this selfie for fun.
- Make this share card.
- Post this one photo.

If an app asks for more access than the job seems to require, check its explanation and privacy policy before continuing.

## How to check an iPhone app's photo access

To review an installed app:

1. Open **Settings**.
2. Tap **Privacy & Security**.
3. Tap **Photos**.
4. Choose the app.
5. Switch to the narrowest access level that still supports what you want to do.

For apps you use rarely, **None** or **Limited Access** is often enough. For apps that only save finished images to your camera roll, **Add Photos Only** may be enough when available. For backup or library-management apps, **Full Access** may be the point of the product.

## What to check before using a photo or aura app

Before uploading a selfie to any photo rating, aura, AI edit, or vibe check app, look for four things.

**1. The permission prompt**

Does the app need your whole photo library, or can you select one photo?

**2. The App Store privacy label**

Apple says App Store product pages can show what data types an app may collect and how that data may be used. Apple also says developers provide those answers and are responsible for keeping them accurate. Treat the label as a useful first stop, not the only proof.

Source: [Apple App Privacy Details](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/app-privacy-details/).

**3. The app's privacy policy**

Look for plain answers about uploads, storage, analytics, advertising, account data, deletion, and whether selected media is stored only when you choose to post or save it.

**4. The claims the app makes**

Be more skeptical when an app presents photo results as serious measurements. A playful vibe score or aura card is entertainment. It should not be framed as a biometric, health, beauty, personality, or dating-compatibility result unless the product can clearly support that claim.

For that distinction, see [Are Aura Apps Real?](/blog/are-aura-apps-real/).

## Where VibeRater fits

[VibeRater Social](/apps/vibe-rater/) is a photo-based entertainment app, so the right privacy question is straightforward: what happens when you rate a photo, and what changes if you decide to post, share, or save something?

The current [VibeRater privacy policy](/privacy/vibe-rater/) says a rating-only photo is sent to the rating service to generate the playful result and then discarded, processed in memory rather than stored. That is the line to look for in any photo app: private one-off processing should be described differently from deliberate posting, saving, or social sharing.

## The best default for photo apps

Use the narrowest permission that still lets the feature work.

If you are rating one photo, editing one image, or making one share card, start with a picker or Limited Access. If the app later needs broader access, it should be able to explain why. If it cannot, that is a good reason to keep the permission narrow or skip the upload.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can apps see all your photos on iPhone?**
Only if you give that app full Photos access. iPhone also lets you choose limited access, select specific photos through the system picker, allow add-only access, or block Photos access entirely.

**What does Limited Access mean for iPhone photos?**
Limited Access means the app can use only the photos and videos you selected for that app. You can review or change the selection later in Settings under Privacy & Security > Photos.

**Does using a photo app mean it gets my whole camera roll?**
No. Many photo apps can work from a single selected image. The important check is whether the app asks for Full Access, Limited Access, Add Photos Only, or uses Apple's picker for one-time selection.

**How does VibeRater handle rating photos?**
VibeRater's public privacy policy says a rating-only photo is sent to its service to generate the playful result and then discarded, while media you choose to post, share, or save may be stored so those features work.
